The container is the complexity

Every question that made this feel complicated is a question about a document, not about families. Relationships don't conflict — documents do. This page argues for deleting the document.

01

Look at what you actually asked

Five questions, all of them hard, none of them about family relationships.

Does that mean only one family tree shareable per space? container question
What if two users share two trees that don't agree? container question
If people make changes in the space, does the original change? container question
How are these conflicts resolved? container question
Two people from different families should complement each other's trees blocked by the container
The tell is in your own proposal. You suggested the tree lives in a space, exports to other spaces, stays linked, and edits propagate everywhere. If edits propagate everywhere and everything is linked, then there is already only one graph — and the container is just a label on it. You described the containerless model; it was still wearing a costume.
And a correction on my part: I steered you away from exactly this in the composability question, on the grounds that one shared graph leaks family structure across spaces. You've since said redaction is an acceptable tradeoff. That retires the only reason I argued against it.
02

Delete the container

There is no tree object. There are people — which the fork already has, already deduplicated across spaces by face_identity — and there are relationships between them. A "family tree" becomes a way of looking, not a thing you own.

With a container

Anna and Pierre appear in both documents. Nothing joins them. The space has to choose, or you have to build a merge.

Without one

proposed
Pierre and Anna are one node because face_identity already says so. Anna's side and Pierre's side joined themselves the moment both were recorded.
One permission rule replaces the entire sharing system: you see a relationship if you can see the people in it. You can see a person if you have an accessible profile for them — your own person row, or a shared_space_person in a space you belong to. That's the resolution the fork already does for thumbnails and the people list. No share table, no link, no consent record, no projection policy. Sharing a family tree becomes sharing photos — which is the thing your product already does well.
03

Your five questions, answered

You askedWith a containerWithout one
Only one tree per space? You must pick a rule and enforce it, and explain it in the UI. Dissolves. A space has people; people have relations. There's nothing to count.
What if two shared trees disagree? You design a merge UI and a conflict-resolution model. Can't happen. They aren't documents. Both people assert the same primitive facts, so there's nothing to reconcile.
Two families complementing each other Needs an explicit "combine trees" feature, with all the merge questions that implies. Free, and the best case. Anna records her side, you record yours, both anchor on identities, the graph joins itself.
Do space edits change the original? Propagation rules, write-back semantics, divergence detection. Yes, trivially. There is only one graph, so an edit is an edit.
How are conflicts resolved? A whole subsystem. There are none by construction. What can still happen is a contradictory graph — two unions both claiming Ben as a child. That's detectable with a query and shown as "needs review", not merged.
Duplicates mostly can't happen either. Key a union on its partner pair: if you add Johan + Marie and your sister adds Johan + Marie, that resolves to the same row rather than two. Deduplication by construction, for the case that covers almost everything. (Two exceptions worth knowing: remarrying the same person, and two separate partnerships where both partners are unknown. Both are rare, and both are better handled as a "these look like the same union" suggestion than a hard constraint.)
04

What the schema does

Six tables become three. Everything struck through exists only to serve the container.

Deleted

  • family_tree (ownerId, name)
  • family_tree_share (treeId, spaceId, canEdit)
  • family_tree_node (treeId, identityId, x, y)
  • family_tree_link
  • projection policy
  • merge / conflict resolution

Kept

  • family_union (id, status, startDate, endDate, createdBy)
  • family_union_partner (unionId, identityId)
  • family_union_child (unionId, identityId)

Plus one user setting for "that's me" — a single identity id, not a per-tree row. Note there is no treeId anywhere: that column was the container.

Moved somewhere better

Gender and "deceased" were fields on the tree node. Without nodes they belong with the person — and person / shared_space_person already carry birthDate with a source-propagation mechanism (birthDateSource, nameSource) built for exactly this. They follow the pattern that already exists rather than inventing one.

05

What it costs — honestly

Three real losses. The first is the one that should decide it for you, because it takes back the thing you gained two rounds ago.

No private drafting

You reversed ownership precisely so nothing was visible from the first keystroke. Containerless gives that back up: record that Johan and Petra are married and everyone who can see both of them sees it immediately. There is no draft state.

Mitigation, and it's decent: a relationship between two people only you can see is automatically private, because nobody else can resolve the endpoints. So your private family stays private. What becomes immediately public is relationships between people you've already shared into a space — arguably facts that space half-knows anyway.

No curation

You can't present a specific, tidy tree to a specific space. Each space sees whatever the people it can resolve imply. You lose "here is the Marais tree, nicely arranged".

Mitigation: a named view — a label plus a root person, pinned to a space — gives the feeling of a curated tree with none of the semantics. Zero effect on data, so no merge problem comes back with it. That's option B in the decision below.

Nowhere to store layout

Hand-positioned nodes (x, y) lived on the tree node. With no container there's no obvious home for them, which pushes the canvas toward automatic layout — and unions make automatic layout genuinely hard.

Mitigation: positions become per-viewer view state keyed on (user, root person). Slightly odd, but it's honest — with no shared document there's no shared arrangement either, and your layout was never going to be right for someone rooted elsewhere.
One authority change worth stating plainly. Without a container, "who may assert a relationship" falls back to: you can create one between two people you can see, where you have edit rights on at least one of them. In a space, that's editors — who can already rename, merge and set birthdays on those same people. So it's an increment on authority they have, not a new class of it. But it does mean a space editor can record something about your family, which is the exact thing you reversed ownership to prevent.
06

What survives untouched

This isn't a restart. Everything we worked out that wasn't about the container still stands.

Unions with status and dates

Divorce, remarriage, half-siblings and step-parents all still work, and "Max is your half-brother" still falls out for free.

Store primitives, derive labels

Nothing relative is stored. Aunt, cousin, mother-in-law computed at read time from wherever you're standing.

Anonymous seats

Still doing triple duty: the divorce model, people with no photos, and redaction of people you can't see.

Canvas to edit, focus card to read

Both surfaces unchanged. The canvas now edits the graph around a root rather than a document.

Only recognised people

face_identity still untouched; the feature still can't mint people.

The real payoff

Relation labels on the person page and the asset-viewer people strip. If anything, easier — there's no tree to resolve first.
